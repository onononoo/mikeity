part 5: the classical world

c. 800 bce – 476 ce (about 1,300 years)

persia, greece, rome, the mauryas, the guptas, the qin, and the han build vast empires. philosophers and prophets ask questions that are still argued about, and the silk roads link east and west.

an age of big ideas

between roughly 800 and 200 bce, in several parts of the world that had little or no contact with each other, thinkers asked searching questions about how people should live, what makes a good ruler, what happens after death, and what the world is made of. the german philosopher karl jaspers called this the axial age, because so much of later human thought turns on it.

why then? possible reasons include the growth of cities and trade, the use of coins and writing, the rise of large states with new problems of government, and times of war and upheaval that made people question old answers.

persia

the achaemenid empire

in 550 bce, cyrus the great, a persian king, overthrew the medes and went on to conquer lydia in anatolia and then babylon in 539 bce. his achaemenid empire became the largest the world had yet seen, eventually stretching from egypt and the aegean to the indus.

cyrus was remembered, even by his enemies, as a relatively tolerant ruler. the "cyrus cylinder" presents him as restoring temples and letting deported peoples return home, and the hebrew bible praises him for allowing the jews exiled in babylon to go back to jerusalem and rebuild their temple.

governing a giant empire

darius i organized the empire into about twenty provinces called satrapies, each run by a governor, a satrap, and paying tribute. he built the royal road, over 2,500 kilometers long, with relay stations where messengers could change horses; herodotus admired them, in words later carved on a new york post office. he issued gold coins, dug a canal linking the nile and the red sea, and built a magnificent ceremonial capital at persepolis. the persians generally let local peoples keep their religions, languages, and laws, as long as they paid and obeyed.

greece

the polis

greece is mountainous and broken up by the sea, and after the bronze age collapse it never formed a single state. instead it had hundreds of independent city-states, each called a polis. they shared a language, religion, the olympic games, and a sense of being greek, but they often fought each other. many greeks also founded colonies around the mediterranean and black sea, from southern france to crimea.

the greeks adopted the phoenician alphabet, adding vowels, and began writing again. the epics of homer, the iliad and the odyssey, were written down around 750-700 bce.

sparta

sparta was a military society. it ruled over a much larger population of enslaved farmers, the helots, whom it constantly feared. to control them, every spartan male citizen was trained from age seven in a harsh state system and served as a soldier for most of his life. spartan women had more freedom than in most greek cities, could own property, and were expected to be physically fit.

athens and democracy

athens took a different path. in 508 bce, cleisthenes reorganized its citizens and created the foundations of democracy, "rule by the people". male citizens met in the assembly to debate and vote on laws, war, and peace directly. many officials were chosen by lot. large juries of citizens decided court cases. citizens could vote to exile a dangerous politician for ten years by writing his name on a piece of pottery, called ostracism.

it was a limited democracy: women, enslaved people (perhaps a third of the population), and foreign residents could not take part. but the idea that ordinary citizens could govern themselves was revolutionary and would inspire people more than two thousand years later.

the persian wars

when greek cities in anatolia rebelled against persia and athens helped them, persia struck back. in 490 bce, an athenian army defeated a persian landing force at marathon. in 480 bce, the persian king xerxes invaded with a huge army. a small force led by 300 spartans held the pass of thermopylae for three days before being wiped out. then the athenian navy crushed the persian fleet in the narrow straits of salamis. the next year the greeks won on land at plataea.

the golden age of athens

after the war, athens became the leader of an alliance of greek states, which it gradually turned into an empire, using the alliance's money to rebuild the city. under pericles, athens built the parthenon on the acropolis. athenian writers invented tragedy (aeschylus, sophocles, euripides) and comedy (aristophanes). herodotus and thucydides invented history as an inquiry into causes. hippocrates and his followers argued that disease had natural causes, not divine ones.

the peloponnesian war

athens' power frightened sparta. the peloponnesian war (431-404 bce) dragged on for 27 years. a plague killed perhaps a quarter of athens' population, including pericles. athens' disastrous expedition to sicily lost a whole fleet and army. in the end, with persian money, sparta won.

in 399 bce a restored athenian democracy tried and executed socrates, who had spent his life questioning people about justice, courage, and virtue. his student plato wrote dialogues starring socrates and founded the academy. plato's student aristotle wrote about almost everything: logic, biology, physics, ethics, politics, poetry.

alexander and the hellenistic world

king philip ii of macedon, north of greece, built a powerful professional army and conquered the greek city-states in 338 bce. when he was murdered, his 20-year-old son took over: alexander the great.

in just eleven years (334-323 bce), alexander conquered the entire persian empire, egypt, and lands as far as the indus river in india. he never lost a major battle. his exhausted army refused to go further, and he died in babylon in 323 bce, aged 32, probably of illness.

his generals split his empire. the ptolemies ruled egypt, the seleucids much of the middle east, and the antigonids macedon. this began the hellenistic age, when greek culture, language, and cities spread across the middle east and blended with local traditions. alexandria in egypt, with its great library and lighthouse, became the intellectual capital of the world. there, eratosthenes measured the size of the earth with remarkable accuracy, euclid organized geometry, and archimedes (in syracuse) worked out the principles of levers and floating bodies. the last hellenistic ruler of egypt was cleopatra.

rome

the republic

rome began as a small town on the tiber river in italy, founded, according to legend, in 753 bce. around 509 bce, the romans expelled their last king and founded a republic. rome was governed by two consuls elected each year, a senate of experienced aristocrats, and assemblies of citizens. over time, the common people, the plebeians, won more rights from the aristocrats, the patricians, including their own officials, the tribunes, and written laws, the twelve tables.

rome conquered italy step by step, often turning defeated enemies into allies who supplied soldiers. it then clashed with carthage, the great phoenician trading power in north africa. in the three punic wars (264-146 bce), rome won sicily, spain, and eventually destroyed carthage completely. in the second war, the carthaginian general hannibal crossed the alps with war elephants and destroyed roman armies, most famously at cannae in 216 bce, but rome refused to give up and won in the end. rome then conquered greece and much of the eastern mediterranean.

from republic to empire

conquest made rome rich, but the wealth went mostly to the elite. small farmers who served in the army lost their land to huge estates worked by enslaved people. the brothers tiberius and gaius gracchus tried to give land to the poor, and both were killed. armies became loyal to their generals rather than to the state. a series of civil wars followed.

julius caesar conquered gaul (france), crossed the rubicon river with his army in 49 bce, and won the civil war that followed. he made himself dictator for life. in 44 bce, a group of senators stabbed him to death, claiming to save the republic. instead there was more civil war. caesar's adopted heir octavian defeated mark antony and cleopatra at actium in 31 bce, and in 27 bce took the name augustus. he kept the forms of the republic but held all real power. he was, in all but name, the first roman emperor.

the pax romana

augustus began about two centuries of relative peace and prosperity, the pax romana. at its height under trajan in 117 ce, the empire stretched from britain to egypt and from spain to mesopotamia, with perhaps 60 million people.

the romans were great engineers. they built about 80,000 kilometers of paved roads, aqueducts carrying water into cities, bridges, harbors, and huge buildings using concrete, like the pantheon and the colosseum. they spread latin, which became the ancestor of the romance languages, and roman law, which still shapes legal systems across europe and latin america.

roman society depended on slavery. millions of enslaved people worked in mines, farms, households, and workshops. some were freed and even became wealthy. but slavery was brutal, and uprisings were crushed, like the revolt led by the gladiator spartacus in 73-71 bce.

christianity

in the roman province of judea, jesus of nazareth preached about the kingdom of god, love of god and neighbor, and forgiveness. around 30 ce he was crucified by the roman governor pontius pilate. his followers believed he had risen from the dead. missionaries like paul of tarsus spread the new faith to non-jews across the empire. in 70 ce, after a jewish revolt, the romans destroyed the temple in jerusalem; judaism reorganized around rabbis, synagogues, and study of the torah.

christians were persecuted at times, because they refused to worship the roman gods and the emperor. but the religion grew, especially in cities. in 312 ce, the emperor constantine converted, and in 313 the edict of milan made christianity legal. in 380 ce, it became the official religion of the empire.

crisis

in the third century ce, the empire nearly fell apart: in about fifty years there were more than twenty emperors, most murdered. invasions, plague, and runaway inflation hit hard. diocletian and then constantine rebuilt the empire as a much more controlling state. constantine founded a new capital in the east, constantinople, in 330 ce. in 395, the empire was split permanently into eastern and western halves. the fate of the west is told in part 6.

india

the maurya empire

india in the time of the buddha was divided among rival kingdoms, of which magadha, in the ganges valley, became the strongest. after alexander's invasion, chandragupta maurya seized power in magadha around 321 bce and built the first empire to unite most of the subcontinent. a greek ambassador, megasthenes, described its capital, pataliputra, as a vast city with wooden walls.

his grandson ashoka conquered the kingdom of kalinga around 261 bce. by his own account, hundreds of thousands were killed or deported. filled with remorse, he turned to buddhism and a policy of dhamma: non-violence, tolerance of all religions, kindness to people and animals, and care for the poor. he had these principles carved on rocks and polished stone pillars across his empire, the oldest deciphered writings from india. he sent missionaries abroad, helping spread buddhism to sri lanka and central asia. the lion capital from one of his pillars is india's national emblem today.

between empires

after the mauryas, india was divided again. in the north, the kushans, who came from central asia, ruled an empire that linked india to the silk roads; under them, buddhist art first showed the buddha in human form, influenced by greek sculpture. in the south, tamil kingdoms traded pepper, pearls, and textiles with the roman empire; hoards of roman coins have been found in south india.

the gupta golden age

from about 320 ce, the gupta dynasty ruled much of northern india. it is remembered as a golden age of hindu culture. the poet kalidasa wrote plays and poems in sanskrit, and the great epics, the mahabharata (including the bhagavad gita) and the ramayana, reached their final forms. temple building in stone began.

indian mathematicians developed the decimal place-value system with nine digits and a symbol for zero. this system spread to the arab world and then to europe, which is why we call the digits "arabic numerals". aryabhata calculated pi to four decimal places and argued that the earth rotates on its axis.

china

the hundred schools

after the zhou kings lost real power, china broke into rival states that fought for centuries. the last and bloodiest phase, from about 475 to 221 bce, is called the warring states period. it was also a time of intense intellectual debate, called the "hundred schools of thought".

  • confucianism: confucius taught that society works when each person fulfills their role with sincerity and respect: ruler and subject, father and son, husband and wife, elder and younger brother, friend and friend. rulers should lead by moral example, not force. education and self-improvement make a "gentleman", a person of virtue, not just of birth.
  • daoism: associated with laozi and zhuangzi, it taught living simply and in harmony with the dao, the natural way of the universe, and warned against too much effort, ambition, and government.
  • legalism: taught that people are selfish and must be controlled by strict laws, harsh punishments, and rewards. rulers should make the state rich and its army strong.

the first emperor

the state of qin used legalist methods to build a ruthless war machine. in 221 bce, its king conquered the last of his rivals and declared himself qin shi huang, "first emperor". he abolished the old feudal states and ruled through appointed officials. he standardized writing, coins, weights and measures, and even the width of cart axles so they would fit the ruts in the roads. he linked earlier walls into a great wall against the nomads of the north, at a terrible cost in lives. he is said to have burned books and executed scholars who criticized him. he was buried near xi'an with an army of about 8,000 life-sized clay soldiers, the terracotta army, discovered by farmers digging a well in 1974.

the han

the qin collapsed soon after the first emperor's death. from the chaos rose the han dynasty (206 bce-220 ce), one of the most important in chinese history. the han kept the qin's centralized system but softened it with confucian values. the emperor wu made confucianism the official state philosophy, and officials began to be selected partly through examinations on confucian texts. the historian sima qian wrote a vast history of china that became the model for all later official histories.

under the han, china invented paper (improved by cai lun around 105 ce), the wheelbarrow, the seismograph, and better iron casting. han armies pushed into central asia, and the envoy zhang qian's travels opened contact with the west. the han was so central to chinese identity that most chinese people today call themselves "han". after the han fell in 220 ce, china was divided for more than three and a half centuries.

the silk roads and the indian ocean

the silk roads were not one road but a web of routes linking china with central asia, persia, india, and the mediterranean. few people traveled the whole way. goods passed from trader to trader through oasis cities like samarkand and kashgar. chinese silk was so popular in rome that some senators complained it drained roman gold and was immodest.

at the same time, sailors learned to use the monsoon winds, which blow one way in summer and the other way in winter, to cross the indian ocean. this linked east africa, arabia, the persian gulf, india, southeast asia, and china in a huge maritime trading network, which would be even more important than the overland routes.

ideas traveled along these routes. buddhism spread from india into central asia and china, carried by merchants and monks. so did diseases: the antonine plague in the roman empire in the 160s ce, possibly smallpox, may have killed millions.

the rest of the world

africa

  • kush: after being driven out of egypt, the kingdom of kush moved its capital south to meroë. it was a major center of iron smelting, and its rulers, including powerful queens called kandakes, were buried under steep pyramids. meroë had its own script, which can be read aloud but is still barely understood.
  • nok: in what is now nigeria, the nok culture smelted iron and made striking terracotta sculptures of human heads.
  • aksum: in ethiopia and eritrea, the kingdom of aksum grew rich on red sea trade, minted its own coins, and raised giant carved stone obelisks. king ezana converted to christianity in the fourth century, making aksum one of the first christian states.
  • the bantu expansion: bantu-speaking farmers, using iron tools, continued spreading across central, eastern, and southern africa. today about 350 million people speak bantu languages.

the americas

  • the maya: in the lowlands of guatemala, belize, and southern mexico, the maya built city-states with stepped pyramids, palaces, and ball courts, such as tikal, calakmul, and later palenque. their classic period began around 250 ce. they had the only fully developed writing system in the ancient americas, a calendar of great precision, and a number system that included zero.
  • teotihuacan: in central mexico, the city of teotihuacan grew to perhaps 100,000 people, with the huge pyramids of the sun and the moon along a great avenue. its influence reached far into the maya region.
  • the andes: the nazca people drew giant lines and figures of animals in the desert of southern peru, visible properly only from the air. the moche on the north coast built huge adobe pyramids and made remarkably lifelike pottery.
  • north america: the hopewell culture built large earthworks and traded goods such as copper from the great lakes, shells from the gulf coast, and obsidian from the rocky mountains.

how we know

  • historians of the time: herodotus, thucydides, polybius, livy, tacitus, and sima qian wrote histories that survive. they had biases and sometimes repeated myths, but they are invaluable.
  • inscriptions on stone and metal, like ashoka's edicts or the persian inscription at behistun.
  • coins, which show rulers' names, portraits, and claims.
  • archaeology, including buried cities like pompeii, frozen in time in 79 ce.
  • papyri from egypt's dry sands, which preserve everyday letters, contracts, and school exercises.

questions scholars still argue about

  • was there really an "axial age"? or is it a neat label imposed on developments that were really quite different?
  • when did the buddha live? estimates differ by about a century.
  • how democratic was athens? and how much did its democracy depend on slavery and empire?
  • why did the roman republic fall? was it inequality, ambitious generals, the strain of empire, or the failure of its institutions?
  • how much did rome and han china know about each other? they traded indirectly, but direct contact was extremely rare.

key terms

alphabet
a writing system with a small set of letters that stand for individual sounds, rather than whole words or syllables.
aqueduct
a channel or bridge built to carry water over long distances, especially the great stone ones built by the romans.
axial age
a name some historians give to the period when great thinkers in china, india, persia, israel, and greece laid the foundations of many later religions and philosophies.
bronze age collapse
the fall of several great civilizations of the eastern mediterranean and near east around 1200-1150 bce.
democracy
rule by the people, either directly (as in ancient athens) or through elected representatives.
hellenistic
describing the blend of greek and near eastern culture that spread after the conquests of alexander the great.
pax romana
the "roman peace": about two centuries of relative peace in the roman empire beginning with augustus.
polis
the greek word for a city-state.
republic
a state without a monarch, ruled by elected officials.
satrapy
a province of the persian empire, run by a governor called a satrap.
silk roads
the network of overland trade routes linking china, central asia, india, the middle east, and the mediterranean.
terracotta army
thousands of life-sized clay soldiers buried with qin shi huang, the first emperor of china.
