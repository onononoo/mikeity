part 6: the middle ages

476 ce – 1450 (about 974 years)

the western roman empire falls, islam spreads across three continents, tang and song china lead the world in technology, the mongols conquer most of eurasia, and the black death kills a third of europe.

what were the "middle ages"?

the phrase "middle ages" was invented by european scholars of the renaissance, who saw the time between the fall of rome and their own day as a dark "middle" period between two ages of brilliance. it is a european label, and a misleading one. for much of the world these centuries were a time of great achievement: the islamic world, tang and song china, and the empires of west africa and the americas were at their height. historians of world history often call it the "post-classical" era instead.

this part covers roughly 476 to 1450, from the end of the western roman empire to the eve of the printing press and the fall of constantinople.

the end of the western roman empire

why did rome fall?

people have argued about this for centuries; one german scholar listed 210 suggested causes. the main factors historians point to are:

  • pressure from outside: germanic peoples such as the goths, vandals, franks, and saxons pushed into the empire, some fleeing the huns who swept in from the steppe in the 370s.
  • civil wars: generals kept fighting each other for the throne, wasting soldiers and money.
  • money troubles: heavy taxes, debased coinage, and a shrinking tax base.
  • the loss of africa: when the vandals took roman north africa in 439, the west lost its richest province and its grain supply.
  • the split between east and west: the richer eastern half survived, while the western half had fewer resources.

in 410 the visigoths sacked rome. in 476, the germanic general odoacer removed the teenage emperor romulus augustulus and did not bother to name a replacement. that is the traditional date for the end of the western empire. but for most people, the change was gradual: germanic kings often kept roman laws, officials, and bishops, and many thought of themselves as heirs of rome.

"the dark ages"?

in western europe, cities shrank, long-distance trade fell, and far fewer people could read. that is why it was once called "the dark ages". historians now avoid the term. it was darker in some ways and places than others; learning survived in monasteries, and new kingdoms, laws, and art developed.

the byzantine empire

the eastern roman empire, which we call the byzantine empire (the people themselves always said "roman"), survived for another thousand years. its capital, constantinople, protected by the sea and huge land walls, was the largest and richest city in christian europe.

the emperor justinian (527-565) tried to reconquer the west. his generals retook north africa, italy, and part of spain, at huge cost. he had all of roman law collected and organized into one code, which later became the basis of law in much of europe. he built the church of the hagia sophia, whose enormous dome seemed to float on light. but his reign also saw the plague of justinian, a pandemic of bubonic plague that began in 541 and returned in waves for two centuries.

the byzantines preserved ancient greek learning, and their missionaries, cyril and methodius, created an alphabet for the slavic languages, ancestor of the cyrillic script used in russia, bulgaria, and serbia today. tensions between the churches of rome and constantinople over authority and practice ended in the great schism of 1054, which split christianity into roman catholic and eastern orthodox churches.

the rise of islam

muhammad

in the early seventh century, arabia was a land of trading towns, oases, and nomadic tribes, mostly worshipping many gods. mecca was a trading center and home of the kaaba, a sacred shrine.

muhammad, a merchant of mecca, began around 610 to preach that he was receiving revelations from the one god, allah, through the angel gabriel. these revelations were collected into the quran. his message was strict monotheism, submission to god (the meaning of "islam"), the equality of believers before god, care for the poor, and a coming day of judgment. the leaders of mecca, whose power depended partly on the pilgrims to the kaaba, persecuted his followers.

in 622, muhammad and his followers moved to the town of yathrib, later called medina. this hijra marks year one of the islamic calendar. in medina, muhammad became both a religious and political leader. after years of conflict, he took mecca in 630, and by his death in 632 most of arabia had accepted islam.

the arab conquests

after muhammad's death, his successors, the caliphs, led arab armies out of arabia. both the byzantine and sasanian persian empires were exhausted from decades of war with each other and devastated by plague. within about a century, the arabs conquered persia, iraq, syria, palestine, egypt, north africa, spain, and parts of central asia and india.

conquered peoples were not usually forced to convert. christians and jews, as "people of the book", were allowed to keep their religions if they paid a special tax. conversion happened gradually over centuries.

sunni and shia

there was disagreement from the beginning about who should lead the community. most muslims accepted the caliphs chosen from among muhammad's companions. a minority believed that leadership belonged to muhammad's family, through his cousin and son-in-law ali and ali's descendants. this group became the shia ("the party of ali"); the majority became the sunni. the killing of ali's son husayn at karbala in 680 became a defining moment for shia muslims, and the split continues today.

the islamic golden age

in 750, the abbasid dynasty overthrew the umayyads and moved the capital to a new city, baghdad. baghdad grew to perhaps a million people. paper, whose making was learned from chinese craftsmen, made books cheap.

in baghdad's "house of wisdom" and elsewhere, scholars translated greek, persian, and indian works into arabic and built on them. al-khwarizmi wrote the book that gave us the word "algebra", and his name gave us "algorithm". indian numerals, including zero, spread through the islamic world and later to europe. al-razi and ibn sina wrote medical encyclopedias used for centuries. ibn al-haytham studied optics through careful experiments. astronomers built observatories and improved the astrolabe. philosophers like ibn rushd (averroes) wrote commentaries on aristotle that shaped european thought.

in spain, called al-andalus, the umayyad capital of córdoba was one of the great cities of europe, famous for its great mosque and its libraries. muslims, jews, and christians often lived and worked together there, though not as equals and not always peacefully.

medieval europe

the franks and charlemagne

of the germanic kingdoms, the franks became the most powerful. their king clovis converted to catholic christianity around 500. in 732, a frankish army led by charles martel defeated a muslim raiding force near tours, in france.

his grandson charlemagne conquered much of western and central europe. on christmas day 800, the pope crowned him emperor of the romans. he encouraged learning, setting up schools and having ancient books copied in a new, clear handwriting that is the ancestor of our lowercase letters. after his death, his empire split among his grandsons, roughly into the future france and germany.

vikings

from 793, when they raided the monastery of lindisfarne in england, scandinavian seafarers called vikings raided, traded, and settled across europe. they settled in england, ireland, and normandy in france. swedish vikings, called the rus, traveled down the rivers of eastern europe to trade with constantinople and baghdad, and helped found the first russian state at kiev. norse sailors settled iceland and greenland, and around 1000, leif erikson reached north america, at a place now called l'anse aux meadows in newfoundland.

feudalism and the manor

with weak kings and constant raids, western europe developed what historians call feudalism. a lord granted land (a fief) to a vassal in return for loyalty and military service. at the top were kings and great nobles, then lesser lords and knights. at the bottom, most people were peasants. many were serfs, who could not leave the land and owed labor to their lord.

historians now argue about how "feudal" medieval europe really was. the system varied enormously from place to place, and the neat pyramid shown in textbooks was never so tidy.

the church

the catholic church was the most powerful institution in western europe. it was organized from the pope in rome down to every village priest. it owned huge amounts of land, ran its own courts, and collected a tenth of people's produce. monasteries kept learning alive by copying books by hand, and ran schools and hospitals. popes and kings struggled for power, most famously when pope gregory vii and emperor henry iv fought over who could appoint bishops.

the normans and the magna carta

in 1066, william the conqueror, duke of normandy, invaded england and won the battle of hastings. the normans replaced the english nobility, built castles across the country, and recorded the wealth of the kingdom in the domesday book. in 1215, rebellious barons forced king john to agree to the magna carta, which said that the king, too, was bound by the law and that free men could not be imprisoned without lawful judgment. it was mostly about barons' rights, but it later became a symbol of liberty.

the high middle ages

from about 1000, europe grew. new farming methods, like the heavy plow, three-field crop rotation, and horse collars, grew more food. the population roughly doubled. towns revived, with merchants and craft guilds. the first universities appeared, at bologna, paris, and oxford. thinkers like thomas aquinas tried to show that christian faith and the reason of aristotle could be reconciled. builders raised gothic cathedrals with pointed arches, flying buttresses, and huge stained glass windows.

the crusades

in 1095, the byzantine emperor asked for help against the seljuk turks, who had taken most of anatolia. pope urban ii called on western christians to go to the east, capture jerusalem, and win forgiveness for their sins. tens of thousands answered. the crusades had begun.

the first crusade captured jerusalem in 1099, massacring many of its muslim and jewish inhabitants. the crusaders set up states along the eastern mediterranean coast. on their way, some crusaders massacred jewish communities in germany.

muslim leaders gradually fought back. in 1187, saladin, sultan of egypt and syria, crushed the crusaders at hattin and retook jerusalem, treating its christian inhabitants far more mercifully than the crusaders had treated its people in 1099. later crusades failed. the fourth crusade, in 1204, never reached the holy land at all; instead it sacked christian constantinople. the last crusader stronghold, acre, fell in 1291. crusades were also fought against pagans in the baltic, and against muslims in spain, where christian kingdoms gradually pushed south in the "reconquista".

china and east asia

the sui and the tang

china was reunited by the short-lived sui dynasty, which built the grand canal linking the yellow and yangtze rivers. the tang dynasty (618-907) that followed was a golden age. its capital, chang'an, may have had a million people and was home to merchants, monks, and envoys from across asia. tang poets like li bai and du fu are still memorized by schoolchildren. buddhism flourished. wu zetian became the only woman in chinese history to rule as emperor in her own name.

the tang also saw terrible violence: the an lushan rebellion (755-763) may have killed millions and left the dynasty weak.

the song

the song dynasty (960-1279) was perhaps the most advanced society in the world at that time. rice farming, with fast-ripening rice from vietnam, fed a population that passed 100 million. cities like kaifeng and hangzhou were huge and lively, with restaurants, theaters, and shops open late into the night.

chinese inventors developed or improved a remarkable list of technologies:

  • printing: woodblock printing (the diamond sutra of 868 is the oldest dated printed book), and later movable type made from clay, invented by bi sheng.
  • gunpowder, first used in fireworks and then in weapons such as fire lances and bombs.
  • the magnetic compass, used for navigation at sea.
  • paper money, the first in the world.
  • improved iron and steel production on an industrial scale.

government was run by scholar-officials chosen through competitive civil service examinations on the confucian classics. in principle, any man could rise through education, though in practice it favored families that could afford years of study.

korea, japan, and vietnam

china's neighbors adopted chinese writing, confucianism, and buddhism, but adapted them. korea developed its own states and, later, its own alphabet (hangul, in 1443). in vietnam, independent kingdoms fought to stay free from china after a thousand years of chinese rule.

in japan, a refined court culture developed at kyoto. a court lady, murasaki shikibu, wrote the tale of genji, often called the world's first novel. from the 1100s, power shifted to warrior clans. the samurai became the ruling class, and from 1192 the real ruler was the shogun, a military dictator, while the emperor remained a sacred symbol.

the mongols

genghis khan

on the steppe of mongolia, a young man named temüjin survived a brutal childhood and united the feuding mongol tribes. in 1206 he was proclaimed genghis khan, "universal ruler". he reorganized the mongols into a disciplined army of horse archers organized in units of ten, a hundred, a thousand, and ten thousand, promoting men for loyalty and ability rather than birth.

the mongols were superb riders who could travel huge distances quickly, and they learned siege warfare from chinese and persian engineers. cities that surrendered were usually spared; cities that resisted were often destroyed and their people killed. the conquests killed millions.

the largest land empire

genghis's sons and grandsons conquered northern china, central asia, persia, russia, and parts of eastern europe. in 1258 they sacked baghdad and killed the last abbasid caliph. at its height, the mongol empire stretched from korea to hungary, the largest continuous land empire in history. it was checked by the mamluks of egypt, who beat a mongol army at ain jalut in 1260, and by the japanese, whose defenders and the typhoons called kamikaze, "divine winds", wrecked the invasions of 1274 and 1281.

genghis's grandson kublai khan completed the conquest of the song in 1279 and ruled all of china as the yuan dynasty. the venetian marco polo claimed to have served at his court.

the pax mongolica

once the conquests were done, the mongols made the silk roads safer than ever before, protecting merchants and running a relay system of horse posts. ideas, technologies, and goods moved across eurasia: gunpowder, printing, and the compass traveled west; so did the black death. the empire split into four khanates, including the golden horde in russia and the ilkhanate in persia, whose rulers converted to islam.

africa

west africa: gold and salt

across the sahara, camel caravans carried salt from the desert south and gold, ivory, and enslaved people north. the trans-saharan trade made great empires rich.

  • ghana (not the same place as modern ghana) controlled the gold trade and was known to arab writers as "the land of gold".
  • mali was founded by sundiata keita, hero of an epic still performed by west african griots. its ruler mansa musa made his pilgrimage to mecca in 1324 with thousands of attendants and so much gold that he lowered its value in cairo for years. timbuktu became a great center of islamic learning with mosques, schools, and libraries whose manuscripts survive today.
  • songhai rose in the fifteenth century to become the largest of these empires.

east africa

along the east african coast, swahili city-states such as kilwa, mombasa, and zanzibar grew rich on indian ocean trade, sending gold, ivory, and other goods to arabia, persia, india, and even china. swahili culture blended african bantu roots with arab and persian influences; the swahili language is a bantu language with many arabic words. the traveler ibn battuta visited kilwa and called it one of the most beautiful cities in the world.

great zimbabwe and ethiopia

inland, the kingdom of great zimbabwe built massive walls of stone without mortar, some over ten meters high, and controlled the gold trade to the coast. european colonists later refused to believe africans could have built it, but archaeology has shown clearly that they did.

in ethiopia, christian kings ruled a kingdom in the highlands. king lalibela had eleven churches carved down into solid rock. in north africa, the historian ibn khaldun developed a theory of how dynasties rise with strong group loyalty and decline as they grow comfortable, one of the first works of social science.

the americas

the maya

the classic maya cities of the southern lowlands, like tikal and palenque, were abandoned during the ninth century. drought, overpopulation, deforestation, and endless warfare between cities probably all played a part. maya civilization did not vanish; cities in the north, like chichen itza and later mayapan, flourished, and millions of maya people live in the region today.

the aztecs

the mexica, better known as the aztecs, were latecomers to the valley of mexico. around 1325, they founded their capital, tenochtitlan, on an island in lake texcoco. they built causeways to the shore, canals, and floating gardens called chinampas. by 1500 tenochtitlan had perhaps 200,000 people, larger than any city in spain. through alliances and war, the aztecs built an empire that collected tribute from hundreds of towns. their religion required human sacrifice, which they believed kept the sun moving and the universe alive.

the inca

in the andes, the inca began as a small kingdom around cusco. from about 1438, the ruler pachacuti and his successors built the largest empire in the americas, stretching about 4,000 kilometers along the mountains. without the wheel for transport, iron, or a writing system like others, they ran it with remarkable organization: a road network of about 40,000 kilometers with rope suspension bridges, storehouses full of food, labor service instead of money taxes, and records kept on knotted cords called quipu. the mountain estate of machu picchu dates from this time.

north america

in the mississippi valley, the city of cahokia, near modern st. louis, reached perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 people around 1050-1150, with an earthen mound as large at its base as the great pyramid of giza. in the southwest, the ancestral puebloans built multi-storied stone "great houses" at chaco canyon and later cliff dwellings at mesa verde.

india and southeast asia

india was divided among many kingdoms. in the south, the chola dynasty built a powerful navy that raided as far as sumatra, and constructed great hindu temples. from about 1000, muslim turkish armies raided from afghanistan, and in 1206 the delhi sultanate was founded. it would rule much of northern india for over three centuries and survived the mongols.

in southeast asia, indian and chinese influences mixed with local cultures. the maritime empire of srivijaya on sumatra controlled trade through the straits of malacca. on java, the buddhist monument of borobudur was built around 800. in cambodia, the khmer empire built angkor, a vast city with reservoirs and temples, including angkor wat, built in the early twelfth century and still the largest religious monument in the world.

the pacific

polynesian navigators, reading stars, swells, winds, and birds, reached the remotest islands on earth. they settled hawaii and easter island (rapa nui) around 1000-1200, and new zealand around 1250-1300. the ancestors of the māori were the last people to settle a major land mass. on easter island, the people carved the famous giant stone statues called moai.

the black death and the crisis of the fourteenth century

a world in trouble

the fourteenth century was a time of crisis across much of eurasia. the climate cooled at the start of what is sometimes called the "little ice age". in europe, the great famine of 1315-1317 killed millions. the hundred years' war between england and france began in 1337.

the plague

then came the black death. a plague caused by the bacterium yersinia pestis, carried by fleas on rats and spread directly between people, moved west along the trade routes of the mongol empire. by 1347 it reached the black sea and the mediterranean. between 1347 and 1351 it killed roughly a third to a half of europe's population, and it also devastated the middle east and north africa; cairo may have lost a third of its people. it returned again and again for centuries.

people had no idea what caused it. some blamed foul air, some the stars, some god's anger. flagellants whipped themselves in public to repent. in many places, jews were falsely accused of poisoning wells and massacred.

the consequences

the plague changed european society. with so many dead, labor was scarce, so surviving peasants could demand higher wages and better conditions. lords tried to hold wages down, and peasants revolted, as in the english peasants' revolt of 1381. over time, serfdom declined in western europe (though in eastern europe it hardened). people questioned the authority of the church, which had been unable to stop the disease.

new beginnings

meanwhile, in 1368, the ming dynasty drove the mongols out of china. from 1405 to 1433, the admiral zheng he led seven huge treasure fleets, with ships far larger than any in europe, as far as arabia and east africa, before china's rulers stopped the voyages. in anatolia, the ottoman turks were building a new empire that would soon take constantinople. and in italy, a new movement of art and learning was beginning. these are the threads that lead into part 7.

how we know

  • chronicles and histories: monks, court historians, and travelers such as ibn battuta and marco polo left written accounts, though they were often biased or exaggerated.
  • documents: charters, tax records, court records, and letters.
  • archaeology, which has been especially important for places without many written sources, like great zimbabwe and cahokia.
  • science: ancient dna from plague victims has confirmed that yersinia pestis caused the black death and helped trace its origin to central asia. tree rings and ice cores record climate change and volcanic eruptions.

questions scholars still argue about

  • did rome "fall", or did it gradually "transform"?
  • how useful is the idea of "feudalism"?
  • what caused the classic maya collapse?
  • how many people did the mongol conquests kill, and did the mongol peace outweigh the destruction?
  • did marco polo really go to china? most historians think he did, but his book has puzzling gaps, like no mention of the great wall or tea.

key terms

caliph
a successor to muhammad as the political and religious leader of the muslim community. a caliph's state is a caliphate.
crusades
a series of religious wars called by the popes, above all to take and hold jerusalem, from 1095 on.
dna
the molecule that carries the genetic instructions of living things. ancient dna lets scientists trace how people moved and mixed.
feudalism
a way of organizing medieval society in which land was granted in return for loyalty and military service, and peasants worked the land for lords.
guild
an association of craftworkers or merchants in a town that set rules, prices, and standards for its trade.
hijra
muhammad's move from mecca to medina in 622 ce, which marks the start of the islamic calendar.
magna carta
the charter english barons forced king john to accept in 1215, which put the king under the law.
monotheism
belief in a single god.
renaissance
the "rebirth" of art and learning based on classical models, which began in italy and spread across europe.
samurai
the warrior class of pre-modern japan, who served lords called daimyo.
serf
a peasant bound to work a lord's land who could not leave it without permission.
shogun
the military ruler of japan, who held the real power while the emperor was mostly a symbol.
silk roads
the network of overland trade routes linking china, central asia, india, the middle east, and the mediterranean.
steppe
the vast grasslands of central eurasia, home to horse-riding nomads.
