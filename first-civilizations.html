part 4: the first civilizations

farming villages grew into cities. cities needed leaders, priests, soldiers, and record keepers. along great rivers in the middle east, egypt, india, and china, the first civilizations appeared, bringing with them writing, laws, kings, temples, and war.

what is a civilization?

historians use the word civilization for a society with:

  • cities with large populations
  • a government, usually led by a king or ruling class
  • people with special jobs, like priests, scribes, soldiers, and craftworkers
  • big public buildings like temples, palaces, and walls
  • some form of writing or record keeping
  • trade over long distances

the first civilizations grew up in river valleys, where floods left rich soil and water was available for irrigation.

mesopotamia

mesopotamia means "the land between the rivers". it lies between the tigris and euphrates rivers, mostly in modern iraq. here, the sumerians built the world's first cities, including uruk and ur. each city had its own king and its own god, worshipped in a huge stepped temple called a ziggurat.

the sumerians invented writing. it began as tally marks and pictures pressed into clay tablets for keeping track of grain and animals, and grew into a script called cuneiform, made of wedge-shaped marks. they also used the wheel, counted in sixties (which is why there are sixty minutes in an hour), and wrote the epic of gilgamesh, one of the oldest stories in the world.

later, sargon of akkad united the sumerian cities into the first empire. after him came babylon, whose king hammurabi carved one of the first written law codes onto a stone pillar. the idea "an eye for an eye" comes from laws like his. later still, the assyrians built a powerful and feared empire across the region.

ancient egypt

egypt grew up along the nile river, which flooded every year and left behind rich black soil in the middle of the desert. upper and lower egypt were united into one kingdom under a single ruler, later called the pharaoh, who was seen as a living god.

egyptian history is split into the old kingdom, the middle kingdom, and the new kingdom, with messy periods of division in between. in the old kingdom, pharaohs built the great pyramids at giza as giant tombs. the egyptians used a picture writing called hieroglyphs, wrote on papyrus, and preserved their dead as mummies to prepare them for the afterlife.

famous rulers include hatshepsut, a woman who ruled as pharaoh; akhenaten, who tried to make egypt worship a single sun god; the boy king tutankhamun, whose tomb was found nearly untouched; and ramesses the great, a mighty builder and warrior.

the indus valley

along the indus river in what is now pakistan and northwest india grew the harappan civilization. its cities, like harappa and mohenjo-daro, were amazingly well planned, with straight streets in a grid, brick houses, public baths, and covered drains and sewers.

the harappans traded with mesopotamia, used standard weights, and carved small stone seals with animals and symbols. their writing has never been deciphered, so much about them is still a mystery. their cities slowly declined, possibly due to climate change and shifting rivers.

later, people speaking indo-aryan languages spread into northern india. they composed the vedas, the oldest sacred texts of hinduism, and society became divided into ranked groups that would later develop into the caste system.

early china

chinese civilization grew along the yellow river. the first dynasty with written records is the shang. shang kings asked their ancestors for advice by carving questions onto bones and turtle shells, heating them, and reading the cracks. these "oracle bones" hold the earliest chinese writing, which is the ancestor of the characters still used today. the shang were also master bronze workers.

the shang were overthrown by the zhou. the zhou said heaven gave rulers the right to rule, called the mandate of heaven, and that heaven could take it away from a bad ruler. this idea shaped chinese politics for the rest of its history.

the early americas

civilizations in the americas grew up completely separately from those in the old world. in peru, the city of caral built pyramids and plazas very early on. later, the chavín culture spread its art and religion through the andes.

in mexico, the olmecs built the first major civilization of mesoamerica. they are famous for giant stone heads, and they may have started traditions later used by the maya and aztecs, like the ball game, the calendar, and the worship of a feathered serpent.

other peoples

  • the hittites built a strong empire in anatolia (modern turkey) and were early users of iron.
  • the minoans on the island of crete built great palaces like knossos and traded across the mediterranean.
  • the mycenaeans of mainland greece were warrior kings, later remembered in the stories of the trojan war.
  • the phoenicians, sea traders from the coast of lebanon, created an alphabet with a small number of letters. it is the ancestor of the greek, latin, arabic, and hebrew alphabets.
  • the israelites formed kingdoms in the land of canaan and developed the belief in one god that would shape judaism, and later christianity and islam.
  • kush, south of egypt in modern sudan, was a rich african kingdom that at one point conquered and ruled egypt itself.

the bronze age collapse

at the end of the bronze age, the great civilizations of the eastern mediterranean fell apart in a short space of time. the hittite empire vanished, the mycenaean palaces burned, and egypt was badly weakened. raiders called the "sea peoples" attacked coasts, and drought, famine, earthquakes, and broken trade routes all seem to have played a part.

out of the ruins came the iron age. iron was more common than the copper and tin needed for bronze, so more people could have metal tools and weapons. new powers rose, and the world moved into the classical age of part 5.
