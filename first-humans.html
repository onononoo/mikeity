part 2: the first humans

in africa, a group of apes started walking on two legs. over a very long time, their descendants learned to make tools, control fire, speak, and make art. one species, our own, spread out of africa and eventually reached every continent except antarctica.

walking upright

the story of humans begins in africa. the line that led to humans split off from the line that led to chimpanzees, and our early ancestors, called hominins, started walking on two legs.

one of the most famous early hominins is a skeleton nicknamed "lucy", an australopithecus found in ethiopia. she was small, had a brain about the size of a chimpanzee's, and could still climb trees, but she walked upright. a trail of footprints left in volcanic ash in tanzania shows that hominins like her were walking on two feet.

the first tools

early hominins began shaping stones into simple tools by knocking flakes off them. these sharp edges could cut meat off bones and crack bones open for marrow. this is the start of the stone age, the longest period of human history.

a species called homo habilis, meaning "handy man", is often linked with these early tools. its brain was bigger than lucy's, and meat from scavenging and hunting may have helped fuel that growth.

homo erectus and fire

next came homo erectus, which was taller, had longer legs, and a bigger brain. it made better tools, like the teardrop-shaped hand axe, and it was the first human species to leave africa, spreading into asia as far as china and indonesia.

at some point, hominins learned to control fire. fire gave warmth, light, protection from animals, and, most importantly, cooked food. cooked food is easier to digest, which may have helped our ancestors grow even bigger brains. fire also brought people together around the hearth at night.

our human cousins

for most of history, there was more than one kind of human alive at the same time. the neanderthals lived in europe and western asia. they were strong, well built for cold weather, buried their dead, made tools and jewelry, and cared for their sick.

other cousins included the denisovans in asia, and small humans like homo floresiensis, nicknamed "the hobbit", on an island in indonesia. many people alive today carry a small amount of neanderthal or denisovan dna, which shows that our ancestors met and had children with them.

homo sapiens

our own species, homo sapiens, meaning "wise human", appeared in africa. early sapiens looked much like people today. they had large brains, rounder skulls, and smaller faces than earlier humans.

over time, sapiens developed complex language, which let them share detailed plans, stories, and knowledge. they made finer tools from stone, bone, and antler, including needles, fishhooks, and spear throwers.

spreading across the world

small groups of homo sapiens left africa and spread across the world. they moved along the coasts of asia, reached australia by boat, and pushed into europe and northern asia.

during the ice age, sea levels were lower, and a land bridge called beringia joined siberia and alaska. people crossed it and spread all the way down through north and south america. by the end of this period, humans lived on every continent except antarctica.

as sapiens spread, the other human species disappeared. we are the last humans left. many large animals, like mammoths, giant sloths, and huge kangaroos, also died out, likely due to a mix of climate change and human hunting.

life in the ice age

for most of this time, the world was much colder than today. huge ice sheets covered the north. people lived as hunter-gatherers, moving around in small bands to follow animals and find wild plants, nuts, and berries.

they built shelters from wood, hides, and even mammoth bones. they sewed warm clothes with bone needles. they traded shells and stones over long distances. they also tamed the first animal: the dog, which came from wolves that lived alongside human camps.

art and ideas

ice age people made the world's first known art. they painted horses, bison, lions, and handprints on the walls of caves like lascaux and chauvet in france and on rock walls in indonesia and australia. they carved small figures and made flutes from bird bones.

they buried their dead with goods like beads and tools, which suggests they believed in some kind of afterlife or spirit world. this is the beginning of religion, music, and storytelling.
